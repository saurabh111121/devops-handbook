Salesforce — Senior DevOps Engineer

Role: Senior DevOps Engineer — the title resolves to two very different jobs that job boards constantly conflate. Read the JD's tooling list to tell which one you're interviewing for.
Company Values (ranked): Trust (always #1) · Customer Success · Innovation · Equality · Sustainability
Culture vocabulary: Ohana · V2MOM · 1-1-1 model (Pledge 1%) · Trailhead / Trailblazer Community
Reality check — which "Salesforce DevOps Engineer" is this? There are two, and the skill sets barely overlap beyond Git and CI/CD concepts:
  • Flavor 1 — Infrastructure DevOps / SRE at Salesforce Inc. Engineering on Salesforce's own runtime (Hyperforce + legacy first-party data centers): Kubernetes at scale, Istio, Terraform, Spinnaker/ArgoCD, Prometheus, Python/Go on AWS/GCP. Titled on the MTS ladder — a "Senior" role is SMTS, "Lead" is LMTS — often posted as "Senior/Lead Site Reliability Engineer." Kubernetes/Terraform/Spinnaker → Flavor 1
  • Flavor 2 — "Salesforce DevOps" (ecosystem release management). Hired by any company that uses Salesforce (or by SIs/ISVs) to version and promote org metadata between sandboxes with SFDX, Git, and Copado/Gearset/Flosum/AutoRABIT. Copado/Gearset/change sets/Apex → Flavor 2
The interview-critical distinction: Flavor 1 treats Salesforce's infrastructure as the product (containers, IaC, reliability); Flavor 2 treats the org's configuration metadata as the artifact being versioned and promoted — uniquely hard because environments are living orgs, not rebuildable servers. This page covers both.

Flavor 1 — Senior/Lead SRE-DevOps at Salesforce Inc. (Hyperforce infrastructure)

Synthesized from real Salesforce careers postings (Senior/Lead SRE, San Francisco & Hyderabad/India), which describe owning availability across a multi-substrate Kubernetes fleet of 1000+ clusters powering Core CRM.

Responsibilities

  • Design, build, and operate services on Hyperforce across AWS/GCP with infrastructure defined entirely as code.
  • Own availability/SLOs of microservices on a multi-substrate Kubernetes fleet (1000+ clusters) including Istio service mesh and ingress gateways.
  • Build and extend CI/CD with Terraform, Spinnaker, ArgoCD, and Jenkins.
  • Build observability with Prometheus, Grafana, Splunk, and Elasticsearch.
  • Automate toil away in Python/Go (AIOps, self-healing) to cut MTTR.
  • Participate in on-call, lead incident response and blameless postmortems.
  • Drive zero-trust and just-in-time (JIT) access security patterns.

Requirements

  • 5+ yrs (SMTS) / 8+ (LMTS) in SRE/DevOps/systems engineering.
  • Production Kubernetes and Docker at scale; service mesh / ingress.
  • Strong Linux internals and networking (TCP/IP, DNS, TLS, load balancers).
  • Coding proficiency in Python or Go.
  • Terraform plus a deployment orchestrator (Spinnaker/Argo).
  • A major public cloud (AWS preferred); distributed-systems debugging; CS degree or equivalent.

Flavor 2 — Senior Salesforce DevOps Engineer (ecosystem / release management)

Interview Process — Flavor 1 (Salesforce Inc. SRE/Infra, 4–5 stages)

Salesforce hiring is decentralized — you interview for a specific "Cloud"/team, not a generic pipeline, and team-matching can precede offer numbers. Tell the recruiter early if you're primarily back-end/infra so they route you correctly.
1
Recruiter Screen (~30 min) — Background, projects, motivation, comp range. Know which team/Cloud you're targeting; avoid volunteering salary history.
2
Hiring Manager Round (~45–60 min) — Role expectations, team fit, ownership, on-call/incident mindset, and values/culture alignment. HM-led hiring decision. Some loops fold behavioral here.
3
Technical Screen / Online Assessment (60–90 min) — Most consistently a timed HackerRank OA (~2 questions, any language). Format ranges from a quick in-browser algorithmic screen to a more take-home-style remote programming test (work locally, submit a full project graded on design/readability). Some report a ~90-min proctored DSA test with harder DP/competitive problems.
4
Onsite Coding (~2 × 45–60 min) — The most heavily weighted part. Live coding in a shared editor (e.g., CoderPad / HackerRank CodePair). Salesforce weighs concrete, idiomatic language proficiency; LeetCode-style problems are less common than at FAANG and skew easy-to-medium (trees, linked lists, strings, hash tables; SRE candidates may get "design an in-memory data structure").
5
System Design (~45 min–1 hr, SMTS+) — Prominent at senior level. Ranges from DB/SQL and low-level design (design TinyURL, an LFU cache) to distributed HLD (Google Drive), scalability, and reliability. For SRE: resilient design, capacity/scaling, failure modes, observability.
6
Behavioral / Values (~1 hr) — Anchored on the five ranked values + Ohana culture. May be standalone or folded into the HM/panel round. (See Behavioral & Values section below.)

Difficulty: Medium overall (some OAs are medium-hard). Timeline: ~3–5 weeks once interviews start.

Flavor 2 — Ecosystem "Salesforce DevOps Engineer" flow (Copado/Gearset shops)

A fundamentally different, less-DSA loop (at consulting partners, SIs, ISVs) focused on the release lifecycle, not backend distributed-systems SRE:

  • Recruiter/screening call → a technical round on Salesforce DevOps concepts → heavy scenario-based questioning (often client-facing at partners).
  • Core topics: Copado (user stories vs pipelines, back-promotion, release management) and/or Gearset (metadata comparison, automated deploy, backup, one-click rollback); Git branching; metadata deployment of Apex/LWC/Flows; destructive changes and mid-release rollback; sandbox pipeline design (e.g., Dev → Common Dev → SIT → UAT → Prod + Hotfix); scratch vs non-scratch orgs.
  • Certifications carry real weight here (Copado Fundamentals I/II, Salesforce Admin, Platform Developer) — unlike core SRE hiring.

Practical / Take-Home Expectations

Practical rounds are usually a short take-home (runnable-ish pipeline/IaC + a README of trade-offs), a live pairing/debug session, or a whiteboard design. Interviewers score reasoning and trade-offs over a polished artifact, plus security (headless JWT auth, least-privilege, no creds in code), idempotency, awareness of platform gotchas, and rollback/failure handling.

Platform-DevOps exercise

  • Build an SFDX + GitHub Actions/Jenkins CI/CD pipeline: headless JWT auth → PR validate-only deploy against a scratch org/CI sandbox with RunLocalTests → static analysis → merge deploys or Quick Deploys.
  • Design a branching + sandbox promotion strategy (git as source of truth, source format, back-promotion).
  • Write a deploy script and correctly assemble package.xml + destructiveChanges.xml.
  • Bonus: delta deploys via sfdx-git-delta.

Infra-DevOps exercise

  • Design a Jenkins/Spinnaker (or Argo) pipeline: build+test+bake, immutable artifact promoted dev→stage→prod, approval gates, automated rollback, canary analysis (Kayenta).
  • Write Terraform for a VPC + EKS (remote state + locking, modules, IRSA, least-privilege IAM).
  • Containerize an app (multi-stage, non-root, scanned image; K8s Deployment with probes + resource limits).
  • Design blue-green / canary; whiteboard an HA, auto-scaling, observable system.

Salesforce Infrastructure — Hyperforce (Flavor 1)

Hyperforce (announced Dec 2, 2020) is Salesforce's re-architecture of its platform to run natively on public cloud — AWS, GCP, and Alibaba Cloud (Alibaba serves China) — replacing/augmenting its first-party data centers. AWS is the primary, most mature substrate; the architecture is deliberately substrate-agnostic. Note: despite some secondary write-ups mentioning Azure, official Salesforce Engineering docs name only AWS, GCP, and Alibaba as substrates. Salesforce still runs on both first-party data centers and Hyperforce and migrates orgs itself at no charge.

Why Hyperforce exists — the drivers to name in interviews

  • Data residency / sovereignty — store customer data in a chosen region for GDPR / local laws (EU Operating Zone, etc.).
  • Elastic scale — public-cloud elasticity vs. slow physical provisioning.
  • Security & Trust — secure-by-default, zero-trust, least privilege, encryption everywhere (co-equal top driver — Trust is the #1 value).
  • Compliance — standardized, code-defined controls meet certifications consistently across regions.
  • Agility — infrastructure-as-code enables rapid provisioning of new regions.

Migration gotchas (frequently probed)

  • Enable My Domain; remove hardcoded instance URLs (na123.salesforce.com) — they break on migration. In Apex use System.DomainCreator.getOrgMyDomainHostname().
  • Switch from IP allowlisting to domain-based allowlisting — public-cloud IPs are dynamic and change without notice. Salesforce: "Do not use hard-coded IP Allowlists."
  • Clients must send SNI in the TLS ClientHello, or Hyperforce returns a default cert covering only *.my.salesforce.com/*.sandbox.my.salesforce.com. Prefer mTLS for integration auth.

Salesforce Platform DevOps — Release Management (Flavor 2)

The deployable artifact is org metadata (XML describing objects, fields, flows, Apex, permissions), not a binary. DevOps means extracting metadata into Git, promoting it through sandbox tiers, and deploying via the Metadata API. The ecosystem has shifted from the org development model (org = source of truth; change sets) to the package development model (Git = source of truth; source-driven, introduced with Salesforce DX in 2017).

Apex test requirements (the deployment gate)

Any production deploy containing Apex runs tests. Test levels:

  • NoTestRun — dev environments only; not allowed for production.
  • RunSpecifiedTests — only named tests; each deployed class AND trigger must independently hit 75% (risky).
  • RunLocalTests — all tests except installed managed/unlocked package tests. The standard prod choice: org-wide ≥75% aggregate + every trigger covered.
  • RunAllTestsInOrg — includes managed-package tests.

Quick Deploy: run a validate-only (checkOnly) deploy ahead of the window; tests pass once; then deploy the validated package within 10 days without re-running tests. Deployment tests run synchronously and serially, so this shrinks the release window dramatically.

Top Technical Questions — Orientation

1. There are two roles both called "Salesforce DevOps Engineer." What's the difference?
Flavor 1 is infrastructure DevOps at Salesforce the company: SRE-style work on Hyperforce and first-party data centers — Kubernetes fleets of 1000+ clusters, Istio, Terraform, Spinnaker/ArgoCD, Prometheus, Python/Go on AWS/GCP. Flavor 2 is the ecosystem role: release management for the Salesforce SaaS platform — versioning org metadata in Git and promoting it through sandboxes with Copado, Gearset, or SFDX. The quickest tell is the JD's tooling list: Kubernetes/Terraform → Flavor 1; Copado/change sets/Apex → Flavor 2. The skill sets barely overlap beyond Git and CI/CD.
2. Why is CI/CD for the Salesforce platform harder than for a typical web app?
Because the artifact is org metadata, not a rebuildable binary: environments are long-lived orgs where admins make declarative changes directly in the UI, so drift and back-promotion are constant concerns. Deployments go through the Metadata API and must pass Apex tests with ≥75% coverage, and "rollback" means deploying the prior metadata state, not swapping an artifact. That's why a specialized tool ecosystem (Copado, Gearset, Flosum, AutoRABIT, DevOps Center) exists, and why Salesforce pushes source-driven development — CLI, scratch orgs, unlocked packages — over change sets.

Top Technical Questions — Platform DevOps (Flavor 2)

3. Explain the org development model vs the package development model. Which would you choose?
In the org model, the org is the source of truth: you build in sandboxes and promote deltas with change sets or Metadata API deploys. In the package model, version control is the source of truth: metadata lives in Git in DX source format, work happens in scratch orgs or source-tracked sandboxes, and releases are versioned artifacts like unlocked packages. I'd choose package/source-driven for repeatable deployments, real code review, and Git-history rollback — but adopt it incrementally, since migrating a large legacy org to unlocked packages is a multi-quarter effort. A pragmatic middle ground is Git-backed org development (DevOps Center or Gearset/Copado pipelines) first, then carve out packages.
4. Scratch org vs sandbox?
A scratch org is a disposable, source-driven org created from a Dev Hub in minutes, configured entirely by a definition file (edition, features, settings), living 1–30 days (7 default), starting empty. A sandbox is a copy of a specific production org (metadata, plus data for Partial/Full), refreshed on 1/5/29-day cycles. Scratch orgs are for isolated feature dev, CI runs, and package builds where you want a clean, reproducible environment; sandboxes are for integration/UAT/staging where you must mirror the real org's shape, data, and installed packages.
5. Explain package.xml and destructiveChanges.xml, including pre/post.
package.xml is the manifest of types and members (wildcards allowed for many types) plus the API version. Deletions use a separate manifest with the same schema but no wildcards — every deleted component is named. destructiveChangesPre.xml deletes before the additive changes; destructiveChangesPost.xml deletes after — which matters for dependencies (e.g., update an Apex class to drop a reference, then delete the field post-deploy). Even a delete-only deploy needs a package.xml carrying the API version. Generate both from a git diff with sfdx-git-delta.
6. Why prefer permission sets over profiles for deployments?
Profiles are monolithic and contextual: a retrieved profile only contains permissions for components in the same manifest, so it's easy to deploy a new field but silently omit its field-level security, leaving it invisible to users ("deployment succeeded but users see nothing"). Profile XML is also enormous and touches everything, causing constant merge conflicts. Permission sets are self-contained, additive, feature-scoped files that deploy predictably alongside the components they grant access to, and permission set groups compose them per persona. Permission-set-based access is Salesforce's strategic direction.
7. How would you build a Salesforce CI/CD pipeline with GitHub Actions and the sf CLI?
Create a Connected App with a self-signed certificate per target org and authenticate non-interactively with sf org login jwt (client ID + private key from CI secrets). On PR: install @salesforce/cli, use sfdx-git-delta to generate package.xml/destructiveChanges.xml from the diff, run Salesforce Code Analyzer, and sf project deploy validate against an integration sandbox with the right test level. On merge: deploy for real, or Quick Deploy the recorded validation for production. Add scheduled drift detection (org-vs-git diff) and full RunLocalTests regression runs, and pin the CLI version.
8. Compare Copado, Gearset, Flosum, AutoRABIT, and DevOps Center.
DevOps Center: free, native, GitHub-only, work-item promotion — right for small admin-heavy teams graduating from change sets, but no CI testing, rollback, or data deployment. Gearset: external SaaS with the best metadata comparison/diff engine, data deployment, backup, drift monitoring — fastest time-to-value for mid-size teams and ISVs. Copado: itself a Salesforce-native managed package with the deepest pipeline orchestration, quality gates, governance, and test automation — suits 100+ dev multi-org enterprises. Flosum: 100% on-platform so metadata never leaves Salesforce — appealing under strict data-residency rules. AutoRABIT: strongest compliance story (masking, SOX/HIPAA reporting, Vault backup, CodeScan) — common in regulated industries.
9. Your org's deployments take 90 minutes because of Apex tests. Speed up releases safely.
Levers: (1) validate ahead of the window and use Quick Deploy — the validation's tests count for 10 days, so the prod window shrinks to commit time; (2) delta deployments with sfdx-git-delta plus RunSpecifiedTests for interim environments (accepting the per-class 75% rule) while keeping full RunLocalTests for the prod validation; (3) fix the suite — remove SeeAllData, add @testSetup factories, eliminate DML/SOQL in loops, enable parallel test execution for non-deploy CI runs; (4) run full regression nightly instead of per-PR. This keeps the 75% compliance bar while decoupling it from the release critical path.
10. How do you seed data into scratch orgs and Developer sandboxes?
They contain no production data, so seeding is deliberate. For small reference datasets, sf data export tree/import tree uses the SObject Tree API (up to 200 records, 5 object types, 5 levels deep, with plan files preserving references) — ideal for versioning seed data in the repo. For volume or complex relational graphs, use Data Loader (up to 5M records, Bulk API, upsert on external IDs) or the open-source SFDMU plugin, which migrates related object graphs resolving lookups automatically. Snowfakery generates large synthetic datasets from YAML; compliance-sensitive teams mask Full-sandbox data with Salesforce Data Mask.

Top Technical Questions — Infra / SRE (Flavor 1)

11. What is Hyperforce, why did Salesforce build it, and what changes for engineers?
Hyperforce (Dec 2020) is Salesforce's re-architecture to run natively on public cloud — AWS, GCP, and Alibaba — instead of only first-party data centers, as a code-defined, standardized deployment model. Drivers: data residency (in-region data for GDPR/sovereignty), elastic scale, security/Trust, and compliance. For engineers, everything is IaC (Terraform) and immutable — you don't SSH-patch servers, you rebuild and redeploy images; you design multi-AZ with cross-region DR; you operate under zero-trust with just-in-time access. For integrations, the gotchas are enabling My Domain, removing hardcoded instance URLs, and moving from IP allowlisting to domain-based allowlisting/mTLS.
12. Design a CI/CD pipeline for a microservice deployed to Kubernetes.
Source commit triggers CI. Build: unit tests, static analysis, SAST + dependency scanning, then build a container image, scan it, tag immutably (git SHA), push to ECR. Package: render Helm charts with the new tag. Deploy via GitOps (ArgoCD) or progressive delivery: promote dev→test→staging→prod with gates. In prod, canary — shift 1–5% of traffic, watch SLIs (error rate, p99 latency, saturation) via Prometheus, auto-rollback on regression, then ramp to 100%. Safety: required approvals, automated evidence gates, secrets from Vault/Secrets Manager (never baked in), full audit logging. This mirrors Salesforce's Managed Releases: canary + staggered groups + risk scoring across dev→test→stage→prod.
13. Blue-green vs canary — when would you choose each?
Blue-green: two full environments; deploy to the idle one, validate, flip 100% of traffic at the LB/DNS, instant rollback by flipping back. Choose it for fast clean rollback when you can't easily split traffic and can afford ~2× infra — but cutover exposes all users at once and DB migrations are tricky. Canary: route a small % to the new version, watch metrics, ramp progressively with automated rollback. Choose it to limit blast radius and validate on real traffic when you have strong observability and traffic-splitting (service mesh/ingress) — the tradeoff is it's slower and more complex. At Salesforce scale, canary/staggered rollout with risk scoring is preferred because blast-radius reduction protects Trust.
14. How does Terraform state locking work and why does it matter?
State is the source of truth mapping config to real infrastructure. Concurrent applies against the same state can corrupt it. Locking prevents that: with a remote backend, Terraform acquires a lock before write operations and releases it after. With the S3 backend you traditionally used a DynamoDB lock table (newer Terraform supports S3-native lockfiles); Terraform Cloud/HCP locks automatically. Best practice: remote backend (never local or committed to git) with encryption at rest, versioning to recover corrupted state, and least-privilege access. A stuck lock can be cleared with force-unlock using the lock ID — carefully, after confirming no apply is running.
15. A pod is in CrashLoopBackOff. Troubleshoot it.
It means the container keeps starting and exiting, with backoff between restarts. Steps: kubectl describe pod for events, exit code, restart count; kubectl logs and --previous to see why the last run died. Common causes: app error/bad config or missing env var/secret (exit 1); a failing liveness probe killing a slow-starting app (fix with readiness/startup probes); OOMKilled (exit 137) — memory limit too low; missing dependency at startup; bad image/command. Fix the root cause — raise memory, add a startupProbe, correct config — rather than just bumping restarts.
16. Explain SLI, SLO, SLA, and error budgets, and how they change behavior.
An SLI is a measured signal (success rate, p99 latency). An SLO is your internal target over a window (e.g., 99.9% success over 28 days). An SLA is the external contractual promise, usually looser, with penalties. The error budget is 1 − SLO (99.9% ⇒ ~0.1% ≈ 43 min/month). It aligns dev and SRE: budget remaining ⇒ ship fast; budget exhausted ⇒ freeze features and invest in reliability. It turns "how reliable should we be?" into a data-driven, blameless conversation and directly supports the Trust value.
17. How does the Hyperforce zero-trust service mesh work, and what powers it?
A service mesh built on Envoy (data plane) and Istio (control plane), CNCF projects Salesforce contributes to. Sidecar proxies handle security, observability, and reliability so app teams don't. Zero-trust means no implicit trust even inside the private network — every service-to-service call is authenticated, authorized, and encrypted with mTLS by default. Blast-radius reduction is engineered via control-plane sharding (a control-plane failure hits only a subset of services) and per-cluster isolation, plus circuit breakers. Salesforce even added WebAssembly extensions to inject custom logic into the data path. It started on AWS but is substrate-agnostic.
18. What is Just-in-Time (JIT) access and why does it matter in a Trust-first company?
JIT means engineers hold no standing production credentials. Access is requested on demand, scoped to a resource/task, approved, time-boxed, fully audited, and auto-revoked when the window expires (e.g., temporary read access to debug a live incident). It's a pillar of zero-trust and least privilege: it shrinks the attack surface (no long-lived keys to steal), limits blast radius, and produces an audit trail. In a company whose #1 value is Trust, protecting customer data outranks convenience — so JIT plus least-privilege IAM, short-lived credentials, and mTLS workload identity are the norm.
19. How do you manage secrets across CI/CD and Kubernetes?
Never commit secrets, bake them into images, or leave them in plaintext Terraform state. Use a dedicated system: HashiCorp Vault (dynamic, short-lived, leased secrets) or cloud-native AWS Secrets Manager / SSM Parameter Store backed by KMS. In Kubernetes, avoid raw Secrets in manifests; inject at runtime via the External Secrets Operator, Vault Agent, or the Secrets Store CSI driver, and enable etcd encryption-at-rest. Principles: encrypt in transit and at rest, rotate automatically, scope by least privilege, audit access. Best of all, prefer identity-based auth (IRSA on EKS, SPIFFE-style workload identity, mTLS) so services authenticate cryptographically instead of passing static keys.
20. How does Salesforce run ~250k weekly production changes, and what happened to Spinnaker?
Salesforce leaned heavily on Spinnaker for multi-cloud CD, but as Hyperforce grew (2,000+ prod services, fleets over 10,000 instances, ~3 changes/second) they built an internal Managed Releases (MR) platform purpose-fit to Hyperforce. MR enforces dev→test→stage→prod with automated evidence gates (coverage, validation) instead of manual sign-off, and uses layered strategies: canary, staggered groups, and a "global" path for emergency break-fixes. A reputation/risk-scoring system orders rollouts so lower-risk instances go first, and a "follow the moon" model releases in off-peak regions first — all to shrink blast radius. Result: only one or two production incidents since 2021 across tens of thousands of releases.

Troubleshooting Framework

Use this structure in EVERY scenario answer:

1
Read the exact error — Deploy result / component errors / log line. Don't guess before reading.
2
Reproduce — Full run in a sandbox (not the Developer Console cache); can you trigger it consistently?
3
Isolate — Metadata dependency? Coverage? Auth? Drift? Diff prod vs git/sandbox for the failing components.
4
Fix & validate — Correct the root cause; run a check-only deploy to confirm before committing.
5
Deploy safely — Quick Deploy the validated package; forward-fix (Salesforce has no native rollback).
6
Prevent recurrence — Drift detection, back-promotion, permission-set hygiene, runbooks.

Common Troubleshooting Scenarios

Production deploy blocked by Apex coverage < 75%

Symptom: "Average test coverage across all Apex Classes and Triggers is XX%, at least 75% test coverage is required." New classes/triggers show 0%, or tests fail during the run.

Causes:

  • New Apex without tests, or tests that run lines but contain no System.assert calls
  • RunSpecifiedTests used — each class/trigger must independently reach 75%
  • Test data setup fails in prod (validation rules, required fields, duplicate rules, triggers behave differently)
  • Async code not wrapped in Test.startTest()/stopTest(), so it never runs
  • @isTest(SeeAllData=true) tests that passed on sandbox data fail on prod data

Fix: Full run in sandbox — sf apex run test --test-level RunLocalTests --code-coverage; query ApexCodeCoverageAggregate for uncovered lines. Add real assertions, @testSetup data, and Test.startTest()/stopTest() around async. Ensure org-wide ≥75% AND every trigger >0%. Validate check-only first, then Quick Deploy. Never disable failing required tests to force a pass.

Deployment fails: "component not found" / missing dependency

Symptom: "In field: field — no CustomField named Account.Foo__c found", or a layout/flow/profile references metadata not in the package or target org.

Causes:

  • Dependent metadata omitted (a layout referencing a field, but the field isn't included)
  • Child deployed before parent (field without its object; record type without its value set)
  • Target org missing a required feature/edition (Person Accounts, Field Service…) or managed package
  • Profile/permission set references a class/field not being deployed and not already present
  • API version too low to recognize a newer type; name/case mismatch

Fix: Assemble a complete, dependency-aware package using tool dependency analysis (Gearset/Copado) or sfdx-git-delta, not a hand-picked change set. Include all referenced metadata; enable required features and install managed packages first; deploy atomically (or use pre/post ordering). Bump the API version. Validate check-only to surface all missing references first.

Git merge conflict in metadata XML (profiles, flexipages)

Symptom: Two branches touch the same .profile-meta.xml/.flexipage-meta.xml and git leaves conflict markers. Even non-overlapping changes conflict because the platform reorders XML on retrieve.

Causes:

  • Profiles aggregate FLS, object perms, layout assignments, tab visibility into one giant file
  • Non-deterministic element ordering between retrieves creates spurious diffs
  • Developers retrieve full, unscoped profiles instead of only their slice

Fix: Migrate permissions to permission sets / permission set groups (small, single-purpose, rarely collide). Use decomposed source format so each object/field/flexipage is its own file. Add a deterministic XML sort on pre-commit (Gearset/Copado normalize this). Retrieve only what you changed; deploy deltas with sfdx-git-delta. Resolve remaining conflicts understanding XML semantics (usually keep both sides' fieldPermissions) and re-validate check-only.

Sandbox refresh wiped data / broke integrations

Symptom: After refresh, custom data is gone, integrations fail (or hit production endpoints), scheduled jobs stopped, email isn't sent, users can't log in.

Causes:

  • Developer/Dev Pro sandboxes copy metadata only; only Partial/Full include data — refresh replaces the org
  • Named Credentials, Remote Site Settings, Custom Settings still point at production systems
  • Refresh appends .invalid to usernames, resets email to "System only", deactivates scheduled Apex

Fix: Automate with a SandboxPostCopy Apex class that re-points endpoints to sandbox, fixes usernames, sets email deliverability, seeds data, reactivates jobs. Keep environment-specific config in Custom Metadata/Settings keyed by environment. Pick the right sandbox type when data is needed. Back up/export before refresh; maintain a documented post-refresh runbook.

Copado/Gearset promotion fails (validation / back-promotion drift)

Symptom: Promoting UAT→prod fails validation; green in a lower env but red in prod; tool warns "component will be overwritten"; target has newer changes than the branch (drift).

Causes:

  • Tests pass in a light sandbox but fail in prod (prod-only validation/duplicate rules, data volume, active automation)
  • Incomplete user story / change set — a dependency left out
  • Back-promotion drift — a prod hotfix never merged back, so the older branch would overwrite newer prod state
  • Expired org credential; target missing a feature/managed package

Fix: Run a validation-only promotion and read per-component errors. Include ALL related metadata via dependency analysis. Perform regular back-promotions (Copado Back Promotion; Gearset compare prod→lower and merge) after every hotfix so drift disappears. Refresh org credentials; validate against a Full-copy staging sandbox mirroring prod data before promoting.

Config drift from manual production changes

Symptom: Deployments overwrite prod changes or fail; fields/profiles/flows differ between sandbox and prod; a comparison tool reports dozens of differences not in version control.

Causes:

  • Admins make declarative "clicks" changes directly in prod that never reach git
  • Emergency hotfixes applied straight to prod, bypassing the pipeline
  • Lower sandboxes not refreshed; managed-package upgrades applied unevenly

Fix: Make git the single source of truth — all changes flow through the pipeline, and any emergency prod edit is immediately back-promoted. Run scheduled org comparisons as a drift-detection CI job that fails on unexpected differences. Lock down who can modify prod metadata (limit Customize Application / Modify Metadata). Prefer permission sets over profiles to shrink noisy diffs.

CI pipeline SFDX auth fails (JWT bearer flow)

Symptom: sf org login jwt fails with "invalid_grant", "user hasn't approved this consumer", "invalid_client_id", or "audience is invalid"; every downstream deploy step is blocked.

Causes:

  • Cert/key mismatch — public cert on the connected app doesn't match the signing server.key, or expired
  • Connected app not pre-authorized — needs "Admin approved users are pre-authorized" AND the user's profile/perm set assigned ⇒ "user hasn't approved this consumer"
  • Wrong Consumer Key; wrong instance/audience (using login.salesforce.com for a sandbox instead of test.salesforce.com) ⇒ "audience is invalid"
  • Private key corrupted in the CI secret (newline/base64 mangling); profile IP/login-hour restrictions; clock skew on the runner

Fix: Verify the cert is unexpired and matches the key (openssl). Set OAuth policy to "Admin approved users are pre-authorized" and assign the integration user; grant api/refresh_token scopes. Pass the correct Consumer Key and --instance-url/audience. Store the PEM as a file-based secret. Relax IP restrictions or trust the runner IPs; confirm the user is active and API-enabled.

Failed production release — no native rollback

Symptom: A deploy succeeded but introduced regressions; the team wants to "roll back," but Salesforce has no built-in undo for metadata already committed to production.

Causes:

  • Deployments are atomic on failure, but there's no automatic reversal of a successful deploy — no metadata snapshot/undo
  • Some changes are irreversible: deleting a field destroys its data; changing a field type can lose data
  • No prior-release artifact or data backup retained

Fix: Forward-fix is primary: redeploy the previous known-good metadata from a git tag through a fast path (validate + Quick Deploy, valid ~10 days). Use destructiveChanges.xml to remove components that shouldn't exist. For data, restore from backups (Salesforce Backup / Own / Gearset / Weekly Export) — a metadata deploy won't restore records. De-risk future releases with feature flags (Custom Metadata/Custom Permissions) so risky changes toggle off without a redeploy; validate against a Full-copy staging sandbox first.

Behavioral & Values Round

Salesforce behavioral interviewing is explicitly values-driven. The company recommends STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result — use "I", ~60% on Action, quantify the Result). Best practice for Salesforce specifically: name the value your story demonstrates, and end with a metric (mirroring the "Measures" discipline of V2MOM). Prepare ~2 stories per value. These mappings are recommended prep, not an official rubric — but candidates commonly lose ground by treating this as a generic behavioral round.
ValueWhat they probeStory that lands
Trust (#1)Integrity, admitting mistakes, transparency, handling sensitive dataYou surfaced bad news early and proactively, before it was discovered, then built a safeguard
Customer SuccessGoing above and beyond; owning the outcome end-to-endResult anchored in the customer's measurable outcome, not your effort
InnovationChallenging the status quo; acting on feedbackAn iterative story with a wrong turn — you spotted an inefficiency, prototyped, and shipped it
EqualityInclusive behavior, amplifying quiet voices, mentoringYou created space for a teammate being talked over, or mentored someone nontraditional
SustainabilityRarely a direct questionCite it in "Why Salesforce?" to show research depth

High-frequency questions (with the value they map to)

  • "Tell me about a time you made a mistake at work." Trust — disclose early, fix it, add a safeguard. Never a fake mistake ("I work too hard").
  • "Describe going above and beyond for a customer." Customer Success — Result = the customer's outcome (renewal saved, adoption up).
  • "Tell me about challenging the status quo / an innovative solution." Innovation — sought feedback and iterated, not lone-genius.
  • "How did you make your team more inclusive / advocate for someone unheard?" Equality — concrete action + outcome for the person.
  • "Tell me about a conflict with a coworker." Trust/Ohana — direct, respectful, understand their view first; warmth under disagreement.
  • "How do you set goals and prioritize when everything's important?" — the moment to show you know V2MOM (Vision, Values, Methods, Obstacles, Measures).
  • "Tell me about difficult feedback you received." — Trailblazer/lifelong-learner: feedback → concrete change → measurable growth.
  • "Tell me about giving back / volunteering." 1-1-1 — authentic give-back; mentoring/open-source counts.
  • "Why Salesforce?" — name the five values (Trust first), connect 2–3 to your experience; reference Trailhead badges you earned preparing. Don't over-lean on "Ohana" as a buzzword.
Culture vocabulary — know it, use it knowledgeably (not performatively):
  • V2MOM — Vision, Values, Methods, Obstacles, Measures. Benioff's 1999 internal alignment framework that every employee still writes annually. It is not a candidate-evaluation rubric — reference it for goal-setting/prioritization questions, not "V2MOM alignment."
  • Ohana — "family" (employees, customers, partners, communities). Note: quietly de-emphasized since the 2023 layoffs, so use it knowledgeably.
  • 1-1-1 model (Pledge 1%) — 1% equity, 1% product, 1% employee time (paid Volunteer Time Off), pioneered in 1999.
  • Trailhead / Trailblazer Community — free gamified learning (badges, superbadges, Ranger rank). Completing relevant badges is concrete "lifelong learner" evidence.
